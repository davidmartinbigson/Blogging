The last table had been booked in 1985, though nobody knew it at the time.

Ferran had been twenty-six then, washing dishes in someone else's kitchen and eating one meal a day, which was the staff meal, which was usually whatever had been cooked badly and for too long. He had decided, while scraping a pan, that he would like to feed people properly for a living, and then he had forgiven himself for the thought and gone back to the pan.

The lease ran out on the last day of October. He had not renewed it. The letter from his knees had arrived long before the letter from the landlord.

## The menu

For forty-one years the menu had been the same size: nine dishes, six of them constant, three that changed with the week and the market and his own mood. He wrote it every morning in the same blue pen.

On the final Sunday he did not write it.

"Chef," said Amaia, the only other person left in the kitchen. "The table is at eight."

"I know."

"Then what are we serving?"

He looked at the empty slate and found that the answer was not on it. "What does the walk-in have?"

She went and looked. She came back and read him the inventory — a short list, deliberately, because he hated waste and had spent forty-one years proving that a small menu cooked properly beats a large one cooked quickly. She read it as though reading the shipping forecast.

When she finished, he nodded slowly, and said, "Then we'll cook what's there."

"That's not a menu, chef."

"No," he agreed. "It's a dinner."

## The guest

She arrived at eight exactly, as she had arrived at eight exactly for thirty-eight years, in a coat the colour of old wine, with a book she would not open. Her name was Marguerite and she had first come when the restaurant had nine tables and no sign, on a Tuesday when it was raining, because the other place she had wanted to eat was closed.

She had eaten here every Sunday since. She had eaten through her marriage, her divorce, her sister's illness, her own. She was the only person in the world who had eaten every dish he had ever cooked, including the failures, one of which she had described as "brave" in a tone that had kept him awake for two nights.

"Chef," she said, and sat down, and put the book face down on the table. "No menu tonight?"

"No menu."

"Good," she said. "I always wanted to know what you would cook if nobody was watching."

## The service

They cooked from memory and inventory, which meant they cooked with everything they had.

The first plate was nothing that had ever been on the menu: cold potatoes, crushed with a great deal of olive oil, salt, and the last of the good vinegar. Marguerite ate it slowly. "This is the staff meal," she said, after a while. "Isn't it."

"It's the staff meal," Ferran agreed, from the pass. "I ate it for four years. I stopped being hungry and started being a chef in the same week."

The second was a fish he had bought that morning without knowing why — a sea bass that had looked at him through the ice with an expression he had disagreed with. Cooked whole, on the bone, with fennel and lemon and nothing clever.

The third was not food. It was a bowl of the soup he made in 1991 for the whole street after the flood, which he had made every year since on the anniversary, and which was, Marguerite said, the only reason she had ever felt like she lived in a neighbourhood rather than on a street.

At half past ten, Amaia set down the last plate and went out to the yard to cry quietly, where she thought he could not hear. He heard. He did not go out.

## The last plate

Dessert, if it could be called that, was the burnt sugar cream he had made for the opening night in 1985, which had cracked down the middle and which he had served anyway because there was nothing else, and because the woman at table four had said it was the best thing she had ever eaten and he had believed her for forty-one years.

He brought it out himself.

"The first dessert," he said.

"I remember it," Marguerite said. "It had a crack in it."

"It has a crack in it."

She looked. It did. She made a small sound — amusement or something adjacent to it — and ate a spoonful, and then another, and then sat back and looked around the room the way you look at a room you are leaving.

"It's the same room," she said.

"It's the same room."

"Forty-one years and it's the same room, Ferran."

"That was the plan," he said, and then, because it was the last night and the last table and there was no reason to be careful: "I didn't have another one."

## After

He turned the sign at 11:20. Amaia stacked the last chairs, and he stood in the kitchen with his hand on the counter where his hand had been every day since he was thirty-one, and thought, with the calm of the truly finished, that he was not sad.

Marguerite put on her coat. At the door she turned and said the thing she had said every Sunday for thirty-eight years, which was: "Same time next week."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he said, "Bring the book."

She tucked it under her arm, went out into the street, and he watched her go down the road past the closed shutters and the one lit window and the scaffolding where the bakery used to be.

Then he went in and washed the cream dish, which had cracked exactly as it always had, and dried it, and put it on the shelf where it belonged.
