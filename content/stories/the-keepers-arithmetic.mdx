The lighthouse had been counting for him for sixty-one years, and now it was going to stop.

Not the light — the light would keep turning, a machine doing what a machine did. But the counting was his. Every night since he was nineteen, Aldo had stood at the top of the stairs with a pencil stub and a ruled notebook and marked each vessel that crossed the water he was responsible for. Northbound. Southbound. Anchored. In trouble. The notebook did not care whether anyone read it.

He had filled forty-three of them. They sat in a cupboard that smelled of salt and old glue, spines soft as cloth.

"Nobody needs the ledger anymore," the young woman from the authority had told him, not unkindly, on Tuesday. She had a tablet and a lanyard and shoes that were not made for the path. "The radar logs everything. You can take the books home if you like."

He had said he would think about it, which was what he said when he meant no.

## One

The first ship of the last night came at 21:40, a bulk carrier riding low and slow, its lights strung out like a sentence someone had forgotten to finish. Aldo wrote *N* and the time, and then, because it was the last night, he wrote the name of the shipping company as well, guessing at it from the funnel.

He had guessed at funnels for six decades. He had been right more often than he had any right to be.

The sea was doing what it did in October: not angry, just restless, the way a person is restless when they have been sitting too long. The window glass hummed. Somewhere below, the new equipment ticked through its calculations with the confidence of something that had never been wrong because nobody had asked it a difficult question.

## Two

At twenty past eleven a fishing boat came in too close to the rocks, the way they always did, the way his brother had.

Aldo put the pencil down and watched it. He did not stand up. He did not need to; the light had already found it, and the boat turned, corrected, and slid away south with the casualness of a man who has been saved so often he has started to believe he is lucky.

His brother had not been lucky. The *Marisol*, February 1978, nine men and a catch of sardines. Aldo had written the time down and then gone outside and been sick over the rail and come back in and finished the entry, because the entry was not finished until it was finished.

He had written nine letters that winter. He could still recite the order he had written them in, which was not the order of the men's ages or the order of their houses but the order in which he had known them.

## Three

At 00:15, a container ship he could not identify, and at 00:52 a yacht, too expensive and too far out. He wrote both. The sea calmed by a fraction, the way a room does when someone finally decides to sit down.

He thought about the word the young woman had used. *Automatic.* As though the opposite were manual, as though the opposite were a hand on a lever rather than a man at a window at three in the morning, counting.

<PullQuote>
He had never once stopped a ship from sinking. The light did that. All he had ever done was notice, and write it down, and be there in the morning if the accounting was needed.
</PullQuote>

## Four

At 02:30, when the arithmetic of the night usually thinned, Aldo opened the last notebook to the last page and did something he had not done since he was nineteen: he wrote a full sentence.

*The sea was the same as it has always been. I have been the thing that changed.*

He looked at the sentence for a long moment. Then he drew a line under it, the way you draw a line under a total.

## Five

The dawn came up grey and grudging, and the first ferry of the morning crossed the water at 06:12, full of people going to work in a town they did not particularly like. Aldo wrote *S*, and the time, and then he capped the pencil and put it in his pocket.

He carried the notebooks down the stairs in three trips, which took longer than he expected, and stacked them in the boot of his car. Forty-three books. Ten thousand nights. A number that had never once been asked for and had never once failed to be true.

The machine behind him kept ticking, patient, certain, unbothered by the fact that the light it was turning had been, for sixty-one years, a way of saying: *I am here. Go around.*

Aldo got into the car. He did not look back at the tower, because he had looked at it enough, and because the arithmetic of a life is not in the looking back — it is in the ledger, and the ledger was in the boot, and it was full.

At the bottom of the hill, where the road turned and the harbour came into view all at once, he stopped the car and took out the last notebook, and wrote one more entry, in the margin, where it did not belong.

*06:31. One man, northbound.*
