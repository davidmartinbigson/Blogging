The shop had no sign, only a bell that rang when the door moved, and a window full of clocks that had all been set to different times on purpose. People found it because they needed it, which is a different thing from looking for it.

Emilio had run it for thirty-one years. He repaired what people brought: watches, radios, a sewing machine, once a carousel horse. And sometimes, when the thing itself was beyond repair, he repaired the memory instead — carefully, reversibly, and with a written receipt.

The woman arrived on a Thursday, in the rain, holding a music box wrapped in a tea towel.

"It won't stop," she said.

"Most things won't," said Emilio, and lifted the lid.

## The music box

It was walnut, small enough to hold in one hand, with a hairline crack in the lid he recognised as a repair he had done himself, eleven years ago, for a man with flour on his sleeves.

The tune was a waltz. It played; the woman had wound it in the doorway, in the rain, without seeming to notice. The comb was warped. Each turn of the cylinder should have caught on the bent tooth and stopped, but the box went on, and the tune went on, and the woman stood very still with her hands at her sides.

"How long has it been playing?"

"Since Tuesday."

That was not what the words meant, and they both knew it. Since Tuesday a person can hear a music box only if someone winds it. This had been playing since Tuesday the way a grief plays: without being wound, without permission.

## The client

She was fifty, perhaps, and she spoke about her mother in the way people speak about weather in a town they have left — precisely, and from a distance.

"She gave it to me in the hospital. She said, *this is the last thing I can give you that still works.*" She paused. "It didn't work then. I had it repaired after. That's when it started."

Emilio turned the box over. There was his own mark on the base, the small scratched E he used to sign his work, and beneath it, in different handwriting, a date.

"Eleven years ago," he said. "Was it you who brought it in?"

"I thought my father brought it."

"Then it was your father," said Emilio, who had learned long ago not to argue with the arithmetic of a family.

## The repair

He worked at the bench while she sat by the window and did not look at the clocks.

The trick with a stuck memory was never the mechanism. The mechanism was the easy part: the bent tooth, the warped comb, the spring that had lost its tension. The hard part was the handle — the small, deliberate act by which a person could take hold of the thing again.

Some people needed a photograph. Some needed a date. One man had needed a smell; Emilio had opened a jar of his mother's paprika and the man had wept and then, visibly, been able to breathe.

For this woman, he suspected, it was simpler and unkinder than that.

"Tell me about the tea towel," he said, not looking up.

"What?"

"Your mother's kitchen. What was the tea towel like?"

The woman was quiet for long enough that the waltz seemed to get louder.

"It had strawberries on it," she said eventually. "She thought it was ridiculous. She kept it for thirty years and every time she used it she said *look at this, I'm drying the dishes with a picnic*."

## The handle

Emilio set the box down, opened the lid, and held it out to her.

"Now you," he said. "It only plays while it is open. When you want it, open it. When you have had enough, close it."

She looked at him. "That's all?"

"Most repairs are that simple and that difficult," he said. "The music was never the problem. The problem was that it was playing without a handle."

She closed the lid.

In the shop, the silence came down all at once, enormous and clean, the way snow settles after a sound that has gone on far too long. One of the clocks on the wall ticked. The rain had stopped. Outside, the street was doing ordinary business.

## The receipt

Emilio wrote the receipt by hand, as he always did: date, item, work done, price. He charged for the comb and the tooth, which came to eleven euros, and wrote a second line, in smaller letters:

*Handle supplied at no charge. Do not wind it before you are ready.*

The woman read it twice. Then she put the music box in her bag, and the tea towel in the bin, and she stood in the doorway for a moment with her hand flat against the frame as though steadying herself or steadying the shop.

"Thank you," she said.

"Come back if it sticks," said Emilio.

But she would not come back; they never did, when the repair had taken. That was the difference between the things he fixed and the people he fixed, and it was, in his opinion, the correct difference.

The bell rang. The door closed.

He wound the clock that had stopped at 4:10, and set it to the right time, which was also 4:10, and went back to work.
