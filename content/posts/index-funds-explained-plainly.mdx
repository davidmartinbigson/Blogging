The financial industry has a remarkable sales record: it has persuaded millions of people that investing is complicated, that professionals can reliably beat the market, and that you should pay 1–2% a year for the attempt. The evidence has always pointed the other way, and index funds exist because of it.

## Start with what a fund is

A fund is a container. It pools money from many investors and uses it to buy a collection of assets — shares, bonds or both. You own units in the container, not the underlying shares directly.

Because you are buying a collection rather than a single company, one company failing does not ruin you. That single idea — diversification — is the whole reason funds exist.

## The difference that matters: active versus index

**An active fund** employs people to choose investments. They research companies, forecast, and trade. They charge for that work, typically 0.5%–1.5% a year, sometimes plus a performance fee.

**An index fund** buys everything in a specified index — the 500 largest US companies, or a global mix of developed markets — and does nothing else. There is no team picking, so the fee is close to the cost of running a computer, often 0.05%–0.20%.

<Note title="Why the low fee wins">
Every year, the market's return is fixed and the entire industry shares it. Fund fees are taken from that same pool. If the market returns 7% and your fund charges 1.5%, you keep 5.5%. At 0.1%, you keep 6.9%. A 1.4% annual difference, compounded over thirty years, is not a haircut — it is roughly a third of the final balance.
</Note>

## The uncomfortable evidence

The SPIVA reports, published twice yearly, compare active funds with their benchmarks. Across most categories and most horizons, the majority of active funds underperform. Over fifteen years, the pattern typically shows 85–90% of US large-cap active funds trailing the S&P 500.

This is not because fund managers are incompetent. It is arithmetic: the average investor, before costs, must earn the market average. After costs, the average investor must earn less. For every fund that beats the market, another must fall short by exactly the difference — minus fees.

## The four numbers that matter

When someone offers you a fund, ignore the story and check four figures.

1. **Ongoing charge (expense ratio).** The annual cost, taken from the fund's assets. The single most reliable predictor of relative performance.
2. **Holdings and concentration.** What does it actually own? A "global" fund that is 65% US and 20% in five companies is not diversified in any useful sense.
3. **Tracking difference.** How far has the fund's return diverged from its index, after costs? This is the real-world test of both fees and operational quality.
4. **Platform costs.** Dealing fees, custody fees, exit fees. A cheap fund on an expensive platform is not cheap.

## A sensible structure, not a recommendation

The most common "boring" portfolio shape used for long-term savings is a global equity index fund, sometimes paired with a bond fund as you get closer to needing the money. The exact allocation depends on your horizon and your tolerance for a 30–40% temporary fall, which is a normal feature of equity markets rather than a malfunction.

There is no universal right answer, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your time horizon and obligations is selling something.

## Common objections, briefly

**"But the market is at a high."** Timing assumes you can identify the top twice — once now, once later. Long-term investors are usually better served by regular contributions than by a forecast.

**"My country's index is small and concentrated."** Then a global fund, rather than a domestic one, is the more diversified starting point.

**"I want to invest in things I believe in."** ESG and thematic index funds exist, and they are still index funds — same low-cost structure, different screen. Check the holdings anyway; labels are loose.

**"What about crypto, gold, individual stocks?"** They can have a place as a small satellite around a boring core. The mistake is making them the core, at a size where a bad year changes your plans.

## The part that actually decides the outcome

Investing success is mostly behavioural. The portfolio that works is the one you do not touch during a 35% drawdown, that you keep contributing to when the news is bad, and that you leave alone for fifteen years.

An index fund does not make you a better investor. It removes the two things that ruin ordinary investors: high costs and the belief that you can predict the next twelve months.
