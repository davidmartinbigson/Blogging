The financial industry has a remarkable sales record: it has persuaded millions of people that investing is complicated, that professionals reliably beat the market, and that 1–2% a year is a fair price for the attempt. The evidence has pointed the other way for decades, and index funds exist because of it.

<Takeaways>
- The average active investor must underperform after costs — this is arithmetic, not opinion.
- SPIVA data shows the large majority of US large-cap active funds trail the S&P 500 over 15 years.
- A 1% fee can cost roughly a quarter of a 30-year balance.
- Check four numbers: expense ratio, holdings, tracking difference and platform costs.
</Takeaways>

## What a fund actually is

A fund is a container. It pools money from many investors and buys a collection of assets — shares, bonds or both. You own units in the container, not the underlying shares directly.

Because you own a collection rather than a single company, one company failing does not ruin you. That idea — diversification — is the entire reason funds exist.

## Active versus index

**An active fund** pays people to choose investments. They research companies, forecast earnings, and trade. That work costs money: typically 0.5%–1.5% a year, sometimes plus a performance fee.

**An index fund** buys everything in a specified index — the 500 largest US companies, or a global mix of developed markets — and does nothing else. There is no team picking, so the cost is close to the cost of running a computer: often 0.05%–0.20%.

<Note title="The arithmetic of active management">
Every investor collectively owns the whole market. Before costs, the average investor must earn the market return. After costs, the average investor must earn less. For every fund that beats the market, another must fall short by exactly the difference — and fees are charged either way. William Sharpe made this argument in 1991 and it has not been refuted.
</Note>

## The evidence

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA scorecards twice a year, comparing active funds with their benchmarks. Across most categories and most horizons, the majority underperform. Over 15-year periods, the share of US large-cap active funds trailing the S&P 500 has typically landed between roughly 85% and 90%.

That is not because fund managers are incompetent. It is because the average dollar invested actively must, by construction, hold the market portfolio before costs and less than it after. Fees decide the sign of the difference.

## The four numbers that matter

When someone offers you a fund, ignore the story and check four figures.

1. **Ongoing charge (expense ratio).** The annual cost taken from fund assets, disclosed to regulators and required to appear in the fund's documentation. It is the most reliable predictor of relative performance.
2. **Holdings and concentration.** A "global" fund that is 65% US and 20% in five companies is not diversified in any useful sense.
3. **Tracking difference.** How far has the fund's return diverged from its index, after costs? This tests fees *and* operational quality at once.
4. **Platform costs.** Dealing fees, custody fees, exit fees. A cheap fund on an expensive platform is not cheap.

## What diversification is not

Diversification is not owning twelve funds that hold the same shares. Nor is it owning a domestic index in a small, concentrated market and calling it global exposure. And it does not remove **market risk**: equities can fall 30–40% and stay down for years. Diversification removes company-specific risk, not the risk that stocks as an asset class will be worth less for a while.

The only things you control in investing are cost, contribution size, and patience. Everything else is weather.

## Common objections, answered briefly

**"The market is at a high."** Timing requires being right twice — once now, once later. Regular contributions beat forecasts for most long-term savers.

**"My country's index is tiny and concentrated."** Then a global fund, rather than a domestic one, is the more diversified starting point.

**"What about individual stocks, gold, crypto?"** They can be a small satellite around a boring core. The mistake is making them the core, at a size where a bad year changes your plans.

**"Isn't this just settling for average?"** No — it is settling for the market return minus a very small cost, which has historically beaten most professionals' net returns.

## The part that decides the outcome

Investing results are mostly behavioural. The portfolio that works is the one you do not touch during a 35% drawdown, that you keep contributing to when the news is bad, and that you leave alone for fifteen years.

An index fund does not make you a better investor. It removes the two things that ruin ordinary investors: high costs and the belief that you can predict the next twelve months.
