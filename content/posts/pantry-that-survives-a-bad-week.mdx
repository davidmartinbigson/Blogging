There is a particular kind of bad week: you get home late, the fridge holds half a lemon and a suspicious jar, and the shop is a twenty-minute walk that feels like an hour. On those nights the difference between a decent dinner and a delivery order is not skill. It is whether the cupboard holds three useful things.

The list below is deliberately short. Ten items, chosen because they combine in many directions, keep for a long time, and none of them spoils in a fortnight.

<Takeaways>
- Ten shelf-stable items produce at least eight distinct dinners.
- Canned low-acid foods keep 2–5 years; high-acid foods 12–18 months.
- Rotate with a use-first shelf; a pantry fails through neglect, not shortage.
- Never store garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature — that is the textbook botulism scenario.
</Takeaways>

## The ten

1. **Tinned chickpeas or white beans.** Protein, texture, and a liquid that thickens.
2. **Tinned tomatoes.** The base of half of everything you will cook.
3. **Dried pasta.** The fastest complete meal in the cupboard.
4. **Rice.** Cheap, filling, and pairs with everything else here.
5. **Olive oil.** Carries flavour; the one item worth buying better.
6. **Garlic.** The cheapest way to make plain food taste intentional.
7. **Onions.** The foundation of anything savoury.
8. **A hard cheese.** Parmesan, pecorino or a firm cheddar — extraordinary flavour per gram.
9. **Tinned fish.** Sardines, mackerel, tuna: a whole meal with no cooking.
10. **Stock cubes or paste.** Turns water into something that tastes like a decision was made.

Add salt, black pepper, a vinegar or lemon, and chilli flakes as permanent residents. They are not ingredients, they are tools — and without them the ten items above taste like ingredients.

<Note title="Why these ten and not thirty">
Most pantry lists are really shopping lists: they assume a large kitchen, a car, and a version of you who dry-fries spices on a Tuesday. This is a *minimum viable pantry* — the smallest set that produces a real meal with nothing else in the house. It fits on two shelves and costs roughly $45–70 to establish from empty.
</Note>

## Eight dinners from those ten

These are methods, not recipes; quantities are forgiving.

**1. Pasta with garlic and beans.** Sizzle sliced garlic in olive oil, add a drained tin of chickpeas, crush a third of them, toss with pasta and a little pasta water, finish with cheese and black pepper.

**2. Tomato and sardine pasta.** Tinned tomatoes, a tin of sardines broken up, chilli, garlic, fifteen minutes. The sardines dissolve; the result tastes of the sea rather than of fish.

**3. Rice and beans, properly.** Onion and garlic until soft, tinned tomatoes, drained beans, rice and stock, lid on. Twenty minutes, cheese on top.

**4. Crispy chickpeas on anything.** Dry a tin, fry in a generous film of oil for 10–12 minutes until they rattle, salt hard, add chilli and lemon.

**5. Tomato rice.** A risotto without the stirring: onion, garlic, tomato, rice, stock, lid on, then ten minutes off the heat.

**6. Pasta with cheese and pepper.** Cacio e pepe if you are careful with the water; a very good cheese-and-butter pasta if you are not.

**7. Tinned fish on toast.** Thinly sliced onion, a lot of lemon, olive oil. Five minutes of work, a proper dinner.

**8. The end-of-week soup.** Whatever is left, a stock cube, salt, bread. Not exciting; reliably good.

## Storage times that are actually documented

| Item | Practical shelf life | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-acid tinned goods (beans, meat, veg) | 2–5 years | Quality fades before safety does |
| High-acid tinned goods (tomatoes, fruit) | 12–18 months | Acid attacks the can lining over time |
| Dried pasta | 2 years+ | Beyond that, texture suffers |
| White rice | Years, if dry and cool | Airtight container; moisture is the enemy |
| Brown rice | ~6 months | Oils in the bran go rancid |
| Olive oil | 18–24 months from harvest | Store dark and cool; heat and light are worse than time |
| Hard cheese | Weeks, refrigerated | Wrap it so it can breathe, not in cling film |

<Note title="The one real safety rule">
Botulism needs low acid, no oxygen and a warm-ish temperature. Garlic or herbs submerged in oil and left on the counter is exactly that combination, and it is the classic home-made case. Refrigerate garlic-in-oil and use it within a few days, or freeze it in cubes. Commercial versions are acidified precisely because of this.
</Note>

## The part nobody mentions: rotation

A pantry fails through neglect, not shortage. Two habits prevent it: a **use-first shelf** at eye level for anything approaching its date, and a rule that new purchases go behind old ones.

- **Cook from the pantry once a week**, even in a good week. Pantry cooking is a skill, and skills need repetition.
- **Date anything you freeze.** A freezer full of unlabelled containers is a cupboard with extra steps.

This also has a measurable environmental payoff. The USDA and EPA estimate that 30–40% of the US food supply is wasted, and food is the largest single category of material in US landfills. A pantry you actually rotate is the least glamorous way to cut that number at home.

## What to add later

Expand with items that multiply combinations rather than items that add single dishes: dried lentils, a vinegar you like (sherry or red wine), frozen peas, tinned coconut milk, cumin and smoked paprika.

None of them need to be bought in the same month. The ten work on their own; everything else is an upgrade.
