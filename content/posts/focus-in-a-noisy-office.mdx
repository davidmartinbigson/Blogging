Open-plan offices were sold as a way to increase collaboration. What they actually increase is interruption: a study of two Fortune 500 companies found that employees in open offices had 70% more face-to-face interruptions and 62% more email than those in private offices, and reported lower concentration and higher stress.

You are probably not going to change your office. What you can change is how much of the day the office gets.

## 1. Own the calendar before someone else fills it

Focus is a scheduling problem before it is a discipline problem. If your calendar is a white sheet, it will be filled with other people's priorities by Tuesday.

Block two ninety-minute windows a week ahead — ideally the first two hours of your day, before the office reaches full volume — and treat them like external meetings. Name them something legible ("Deep work: Q4 report") rather than "Focus", so colleagues can see they are real.

<Note title="The 9 a.m. advantage">
The single most effective change most people can make is arriving thirty to forty-five minutes before the general start time. That window is quiet, uninterrupted and unclaimed. It is worth more than any productivity app.
</Note>

## 2. Set a default state, not a task list

A to-do list tells you what to do; it does not protect you while you do it. What protects you is a **default**: the state your attention returns to when the interruption ends.

Set one deliverable per block, written as a finished object — "the introduction, drafted", "the spreadsheet, reconciled", "the email, sent". Then, when a colleague interrupts, you return to that object rather than to a screen full of options.

### The two-minute re-entry habit

Make a note before you leave a task, in the file itself, of the next physical action: "next: paste the table, then check the totals". Interruptions cost far less when the resume point is explicit.

## 3. Make interruptions slightly harder — politely

You cannot eliminate interruptions, but you can reduce their default speed. Small friction is usually enough.

- **Headphones.** Worn, with something playing, they are a social signal as much as a sound barrier.
- **Status message.** "Heads down until 11:30, ping me after" in your chat status reduces casual pings significantly.
- **Seat position.** Facing a wall rather than a walkway reduces peripheral interruptions, which are the most costly because they do not feel rude enough to deflect.
- **Batch your own questions.** Instead of messaging a colleague five times before lunch, collect them. You are also somebody else's interruption.

## 4. Protect the exit, not just the entry

Most people plan their morning and lose their afternoon. The last ninety minutes of a working day are unusually valuable, because the office empties and the cost of a context switch rises.

Use the final block for the work that requires continuity — writing, reviewing, planning — rather than for email. Then close with ten minutes of shutdown: what moved, what is next, and what tomorrow's first block will contain. That single habit reduces the "still thinking about work at 11 p.m." problem more than any wind-down routine.

## What to do when the office is genuinely unusable

Some environments — sales floors, newsrooms, a team in the middle of a launch — cannot be made quiet. In that case, change location rather than fight.

- **Book a meeting room for yourself.** Two people can defend a room; one person cannot defend a desk.
- **Use the commute differently.** A train ride with the laptop closed, reading a document, is often the most focused hour of the day.
- **Negotiate one day.** If the role permits, one remote day a week is usually enough to absorb the work that requires uninterrupted attention.
- **Escalate with numbers, not feelings.** "My three biggest deliverables each need a two-hour block; I can get one of them done on Wednesdays if I work from home" is a business case. "It's too loud" is a complaint.

<PullQuote cite="A useful reframe">
You are not being antisocial by protecting two hours a day. You are making the difference between attending work and doing it — and your colleagues benefit from the second one.
</PullQuote>

## A one-page system

If you take nothing else from this piece, take the structure:

1. **Two blocks of ninety minutes**, in the calendar, named after the deliverable.
2. **One object per block**, described as something finished.
3. **A note to your future self** at the end of each block: the next physical action.
4. **Headphones plus a status message** during the block.
5. **A ten-minute shutdown** at the end of the day.

None of it is impressive. All of it is repeatable. That is the entire trick: the people who appear to focus effortlessly are usually just protecting a boring, recurring slot that everyone else has given up on.
