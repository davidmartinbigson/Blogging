Open-plan offices were sold as a way to increase collaboration. The most careful field study of the change suggests they do something else: they replace conversation with messaging, and they raise the number of interruptions while lowering the quality of attention between them.

You are probably not going to redesign the floor plan. You can, however, control four things that decide whether you get any real work done.

<Takeaways>
- Block two 90-minute windows in the calendar and name them after a deliverable, not 'focus'.
- Work from a written 'next action' so an interruption costs seconds, not minutes.
- Interruptions cost far more than their length — batch your own messages too.
- Protect the last 90 minutes of the day; it is when the office empties.
</Takeaways>

## What the research actually found

Bernstein and Turban, using sociometric badges that measured face-to-face interaction and electronic communication objectively, tracked employees moving from cubicles into an open plan. Face-to-face interaction dropped by roughly **70%**, while email and messaging traffic rose substantially.

The interpretation is uncomfortable for the design brief: rather than sparking collaboration, the open office appears to have driven people into channels where they could have a conversation without an audience. Meanwhile, the number of *available* interruptions rose.

<Note title="The 23-minute figure">
In an observational study of working days, Mark, Gonzalez and Harris found it took around **23 minutes** to return to an interrupted task. Treat it as an order of magnitude rather than a law — the point is that an interruption's true cost is set by re-entry time, not its own duration.
</Note>

## 1. Own the calendar before someone else fills it

Focus is a scheduling problem before it is a discipline problem. A blank calendar will be filled by other people's priorities by Tuesday.

Block two ninety-minute windows a week ahead — ideally the first two hours of your day, before the office reaches full volume — and treat them like external meetings. Name them something legible ("Deep work: Q4 report") rather than "Focus", so colleagues can see they are commitments.

The last ninety minutes of the day are equally valuable, because the office empties.

## 2. Set a default state, not a to-do list

A list tells you what to do; it does not protect you while you do it. What protects you is a **default**: the state attention returns to when an interruption ends.

Write one deliverable per block as a finished object — "the introduction, drafted", "the spreadsheet, reconciled", "the email, sent". Then, when a colleague interrupts, you return to that object rather than to a screen full of options.

### The two-minute re-entry habit

Before you leave a task, write the next physical action in the file itself: "next: paste the table, then check the totals". Interruptions cost far less when the resume point is explicit. This is the practical answer to attention residue — you are reducing the unfinished-task background load that keeps pulling attention back.

## 3. Add friction, politely

You cannot eliminate interruptions, but you can slow their default rate.

- **Headphones.** Worn, with something playing, they are a visible signal as much as a sound barrier.
- **Status message.** "Heads down until 11:30, ping me after" reduces casual pings measurably.
- **Seat position.** Facing a wall rather than a walkway reduces peripheral interruptions, which are the most costly because they do not feel rude enough to deflect.
- **Batch your own questions.** Five messages before lunch become one. You are somebody else's interruption too.

## 4. Make the case with numbers, not feelings

If the environment is genuinely unusable — a sales floor, a newsroom, a launch week — negotiate with a business case rather than a complaint:

> "My three biggest deliverables each need a two-hour block. I can get one of them done if I work from home on Wednesdays."

- **Book a room for yourself.** Two people can defend a room; one person cannot defend a desk.
- **Use the commute differently.** A train ride with the laptop closed, reading a document, is often the most focused hour available.
- **Ask for one remote day**, not five. One day absorbs most work that requires uninterrupted attention.

<PullQuote cite="A useful reframe">
You are not being antisocial by protecting two hours a day. You are making the difference between attending work and doing it — and your colleagues benefit from the second one.
</PullQuote>

## Is a noisy office actually worse for thinking?

Partly, and specifically. Research on office noise distinguishes between *sound level* and *irrelevant speech*: performance is impaired more by intelligible conversation, which competes with the language processing your task needs, than by steady background noise. That is why music with lyrics can be worse than instrumental, and why a nearby phone conversation is more disruptive than a busy street.

If you cannot control the room, control the input: masking sound without lyrics, or earplugs plus headphones without playback, usually beats music in a shared space.

## The one-page system

1. **Two blocks of ninety minutes**, in the calendar, named after the deliverable.
2. **One object per block**, described as something finished.
3. **A written next action** at the end of each block.
4. **Headphones plus a status message** during the block.
5. **A ten-minute shutdown** at the end of the day, listing tomorrow's first block.

None of it is impressive. All of it is repeatable — and the people who appear to focus effortlessly are usually just protecting a boring, recurring slot that everyone else gave up on.
