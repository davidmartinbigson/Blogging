The phrase "sleep debt" borrows from banking, and the metaphor misleads in one expensive way: it implies a ledger you can settle. Spend six hours a night all week, sleep eleven on Sunday, balance restored.

Human physiology does not settle accounts like that. What the research shows is closer to a bruise — some of it fades unaided, but a long lie-in does not undo the week.

<Takeaways>
- Adults need at least 7 hours; AASM and the Sleep Research Society state this explicitly.
- Chronic restriction impairs attention faster than you notice it — self-ratings stay nearly normal while performance falls.
- Recovery sleep helps with sleepiness and inflammation but does not fully restore vigilance.
- A fixed wake time, morning daylight and a caffeine cut-off do more than any weekend lie-in.
</Takeaways>

## The two-week study that changed the guidance

The most cited experiment on this is Van Dongen and colleagues (2003). Participants were assigned to four, six or eight hours in bed per night for fourteen days, with cognitive testing throughout. The six-hour group — a completely normal schedule for a working adult — showed steadily worsening performance on a psychomotor vigilance task, ending up roughly equivalent to one to two nights of total sleep deprivation.

The finding that matters most is the second one: **the participants did not feel correspondingly worse.** Their self-rated sleepiness rose only modestly while their measured attention kept degrading. People adapt to the feeling of being tired, but the impairment keeps accumulating.

Belenky and colleagues published a parallel study the same year with the same shape of result: performance declined in a dose-dependent way with the amount of nightly sleep lost, and recovery did not happen in a single night.

## What short sleep actually changes

Beyond attention, controlled restriction studies consistently report:

- **Appetite regulation.** In a 1999 Lancet study, men restricted to four hours a night for six nights showed lower leptin (the satiety signal) and higher ghrelin (the hunger signal), with increased appetite — particularly for calorie-dense food.
- **Insulin sensitivity.** Five nights of five hours in bed reduced insulin sensitivity by roughly 20% in healthy young adults in work by Leproult and Van Cauter, a change comparable to gaining several kilograms.
- **Mood and emotional reactivity.** Sleep-deprived participants show amplified amygdala response to negative images, with weaker prefrontal regulation.

None of this is a reason to panic after one bad night. It is a reason to treat a fortnight of six-hour nights as a health event rather than a personality trait.

## Why the Sunday lie-in cannot fix it

There are two separate systems at work.

**Sleep pressure** is a chemical build-up — adenosine — that rises while you are awake and falls while you sleep. A long night genuinely reduces it.

**The circadian clock** is a daily rhythm that expects light, meals and activity at predictable times. Sleeping from 2 a.m. to 11 a.m. on Sunday shifts that clock later, which is why Sunday-night insomnia is so common: you have given yourself a mild case of jet lag at the exact moment you need to be rested.

So a late Saturday helps with pressure and hurts with rhythm. Recovery sleep is not worthless — restorative studies show it reduces sleepiness and some inflammatory markers — but it is a partial refund, not repayment. Work by Pejovic and colleagues found that even after extended recovery sleep following chronic restriction, sustained attention and some metabolic markers had not fully returned.

<Note title="Red flags for a sleep disorder">
Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, falling asleep while driving, and unrefreshing sleep despite adequate hours all warrant assessment for obstructive sleep apnoea. Sleep-debt advice does not fix a breathing problem, and no amount of scheduling will.
</Note>

## What actually repairs a broken week

<Steps>
<Step title="Anchor the wake time first">Bedtime is an output. You cannot force sleep, but you can choose when you get up. Hold the same wake time within about an hour, seven days a week — this stabilises the clock that governs evening sleepiness.</Step>
<Step title="Get light early, dim it late">Ten to twenty minutes outdoors in the morning is the strongest available signal to your circadian clock. Warm, low light in the evening sends the opposite message. This matters more than bedroom temperature, which sleep-hygiene lists obsess over.</Step>
<Step title="Cap catch-up sleep at 60–90 minutes">If you must sleep in, keep it under an hour and a half later than your weekday wake time. You get a top-up without dragging the clock out of alignment.</Step>
<Step title="Schedule caffeine like a drug">Half-life averages about five hours, with wide individual variation. Stop caffeine eight to ten hours before bed for one week and see what changes — most people underestimate this effect.</Step>
<Step title="Nap short, nap early">Ten to twenty minutes in the early afternoon improves alertness without grogginess. Longer naps, or naps after 3 p.m., make the evening harder.</Step>
</Steps>

## The honest summary

Sleep debt is real, but it is not a ledger. Chronic short sleep costs attention, mood, appetite regulation and glucose handling — and it does so faster than your sense of tiredness will report.

The intervention that works is boring: a fixed wake time, morning daylight, a caffeine cut-off, and a dark, cool bedroom. Repeated, not heroic.
