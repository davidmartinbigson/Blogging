Lisbon rewards walking and punishes over-planning. The city sits on seven hills above the Tagus, and almost every route is a climb or a descent — which means the most useful planning decision is not *where* to go but *in what order*.

This itinerary assumes three full days, a mid-range budget, and a preference for eating well over seeing everything.

<Takeaways>
- Go uphill by tram or funicular and walk down, not the other way around.
- Belém is a half-day, not an afternoon — book the monastery ticket in advance.
- The free viewpoints (miradouros) are the city's best attraction.
- Eat the prato do dia at lunch, when Portuguese kitchens are at their most generous.
</Takeaways>

## Before you arrive

- **Neighbourhood.** Baixa and Chiado are central and flat but pricier. Alfama is atmospheric and steep. Príncipe Real is quieter and walkable to most of the centre. Saving €20 a night to stay far out usually costs more in transport and lost time.
- **Transit card.** Buy a rechargeable Viva Viagem card at any metro station (the card itself costs about €0.50) and top it up as you go. Single journeys are around €1.85; a 24-hour pass is worth it only on days with four or more trips.
- **Shoes.** Cobblestones plus hills. This is not the trip for new leather soles.
- **Note on prices.** Fares, museum tickets and menu prices change; treat the figures here as planning guidance and confirm current prices with the operator before you travel.

<Note title="Why the city looks the way it does">
On 1 November 1755 — All Saints' Day, when most of the city was in church — an earthquake estimated at magnitude 8.5 or higher struck off the coast, followed by a tsunami and fires that burned for days. The Baixa you walk through today is the rebuild: a grid of streets, standardised façades, and the *gaiola pombalina*, a timber cage inside the masonry designed to flex rather than collapse. It is one of the earliest large-scale seismic-resistant building systems.
</Note>

## Day one: Alfama, the castle, and a viewpoint at golden hour

Start high and walk down.

**Morning — Castelo de São Jorge.** Arrive at opening to avoid the cruise crowds. The castle itself is modest; the terrace over the river and the red roofs is the point. The hill has been fortified since the Moorish period and was taken in the 1147 siege that brought Lisbon under Afonso Henriques.

**Late morning — Alfama on foot.** Wander down through Largo das Portas do Sol and the National Pantheon (Santa Engrácia), past squares where laundry hangs across the street. Alfama survived the 1755 earthquake largely intact, which is why its street plan feels medieval while Baixa feels engineered.

**Lunch — a tasca, not a view.** Look for *prato do dia*: grilled fish, rice and salad for roughly €8–14. Plastic chairs and a menu in Portuguese are good signs.

**Afternoon — the riverfront.** Walk from Santa Apolónia towards Praça do Comércio. The Time Out Market (opened 2014) is good for a single convenient snack and about 40% more expensive than a neighbourhood tasca.

**Evening — fado in Alfama.** Fado was added to UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2011. Book a small house — under about forty seats — with dinner included, typically €35–55 per person with wine. Amplification in larger venues removes the reason to go.

## Day two: Belém in the morning, Bairro Alto at night

**Morning — Belém.** Take tram 15 from Praça da Figueira, or the train from Cais do Sodré, which is faster and less crowded. Go first to **Pastéis de Belém**, where the shop has sold custard tarts made to a recipe attributed to the Jerónimos monks since 1837; the queue moves quickly, and the sit-down rooms at the back are worth the extra euro.

Then the **Jerónimos Monastery**, a Manueline masterpiece listed by UNESCO in 1983 together with the Belém Tower. Book a timed entry; the cloister is the part to linger in. The tower's interior is far smaller than its postcard suggests — if queues are long, choose the monastery.

**Afternoon — MAAT and the river.** The Museum of Art, Architecture and Technology (opened 2016, designed by AL_A) sits on the water with a roof you can walk on. Even without a ticket, the building and riverside path are excellent.

**Evening — Bairro Alto.** The neighbourhood invented the Lisbon evening: cheap drinks on the street from about 8 p.m., dinner after 21:30, out past midnight. A glass of wine at a hole-in-the-wall is €3–5.

<PullQuote>
Lisbon does not have a nightlife district so much as a nightly migration: Bairro Alto to stand outside with a plastic cup, Cais do Sodré to dance, and a taxi home that costs less than your last two drinks.
</PullQuote>

## Day three: markets, viewpoints and a slow finish

**Morning — Campo de Ourique market.** Fewer visitors than Time Out, better coffee, and the sense of a city going about its Saturday.

**Midday — Príncipe Real.** Independent shops, the garden with its enormous cedar, lunch at a café facing the park.

**Afternoon — the miradouro crawl.** The viewpoints are free and they are the city's best attraction. São Pedro de Alcântara faces the castle; Graça has a kiosk and the best sunset crowd; Senhora do Monte is the highest and usually quietest. End at Graça for the last hour of light.

**Last dinner — seafood.** Find a marisqueira away from the main squares: grilled sardines in season, *amêijoas à Bulhão Pato* (clams with garlic and coriander), vinho verde. Expect €25–35 per person with wine.

<Note title="If you have a fourth day">
Sintra deserves a full day, and a weekday. Pena Palace and Quinta da Regaleira are the two worth your time; the Moorish Castle is a fine view and a crowded one. The train from Rossio takes about 40 minutes and costs a few euros return.
</Note>

## Eating well without trying

- **Lunch is the main meal.** Portuguese kitchens serve generous *prato do dia* at lunch for roughly half the evening price.
- **Couvert is charged.** Bread, olives and butter that appear unrequested are billable (€2–4). You may decline them.
- **Coffee is cheap.** An espresso at the counter is around €1. Drinking it standing is the local method.
- **Tipping is modest.** Round up, or leave about 5% for good service.
- **Tap water is safe.** Ask for *água da torneira* if you do not want a bottle.

## What to skip

The Santa Justa Lift queue (the 1902 iron lift by Raoul Mesnier du Ponsard is beautiful from outside; the view is comparable to free miradouros), Time Out Market as a dinner destination, and any restaurant with photographs of the food and someone outside inviting you in. In a city this good at food, those three shortcuts are always a mistake.
