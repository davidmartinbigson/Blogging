There is a genre of advice that says the answer to your phone is a screen-time limit, a greyscale filter and a small amount of shame. It rarely works, and that is not a personal failing. Willpower is a terrible interface.

The approach that works is structural. Change the environment once, in a calm moment, so that the decision you would prefer becomes the default rather than a daily battle.

<Takeaways>
- Turn off every notification except messages from people and genuine time-critical alerts.
- Use unique passwords in a manager — and stop forcing periodic changes.
- Prefer an authenticator app or passkey to SMS codes.
- Batch your own interruptions; each one costs far more than its length.
</Takeaways>

## 1. Notifications: keep only humans

This is the highest-return change on the list, and it takes four minutes.

Keep two categories: **messages from real people** and **genuinely time-critical** (a delivery, a flight, a bank alert). Turn off likes, follows, trending, news pushes, promotional nudges and "we miss you" messages.

<Note title="The test">
For each app: if this stopped notifying me entirely, what would break? If the honest answer is "nothing", it is not a notification — it is advertising.
</Note>

Both major platforms now let you batch rather than eliminate: iOS has Scheduled Summary, which delivers non-urgent notifications in two grouped drops a day, and Android has per-app notification channels you can mute individually. Ten minutes in settings buys a quieter month.

## 2. Move the icons

Placement beats intention. Move reflexively-opened apps off the first screen — into a folder, into the app library, or off the phone entirely.

A workable rule: **if you opened an app more than twenty times this week without deciding to, remove it from the phone.** You can still use the website, and typing an address converts a reflex into a decision.

## 3. The interruption math

Notifications are not free. In field studies of office workers, people took around **23 minutes** to resume an interrupted task, and often worked faster afterwards to compensate — at the cost of stress. A handful of notifications per hour is not a small tax; it is most of the morning.

That single number justifies batching: answer messages at two or three fixed points rather than continuously. You will be slower to reply and faster to finish.

## 4. Passwords and second factors

Security and attention are the same problem seen from two angles. An account you cannot recover owns you.

- **Use a password manager.** One manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, KeePassXC, or the built-in Apple and Google options) and unique passwords everywhere. This removes most account-takeover risk in a single step.
- **Stop rotating passwords on a schedule.** NIST's guidance (SP 800-63B) explicitly discourages forced periodic changes, which tend to produce weak predictable variations. Change a password when a service reports a breach.
- **Prefer app-based second factors.** NIST classes SMS as a *restricted* authenticator because of SIM-swap and interception risk. The FBI has repeatedly warned about SIM-swap attacks. Use an authenticator app, a passkey, or a hardware key for email, banking and your main social account.
- **Check exposure.** Search your address at Have I Been Pwned, then change the passwords for anything listed — after which, stop reusing anything.
- **Secure the recovery route.** For your primary email account, confirm the recovery address and phone number are current. It is the master key to everything else.

## 5. Reduce what is tracked about you

You will not become anonymous, and chasing that is a distraction. A smaller, tidier footprint is achievable in one sitting.

| Setting | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Limit ad tracking / reset advertising ID | Reduces cross-app profiling at almost no cost |
| Browser: block third-party cookies | Stops most cross-site tracking by default |
| App permission audit | Photo, location and microphone access are often unnecessary |
| Location: "while using" not "always" | Prevents background location logging |
| App Tracking Transparency / equivalent | Opt out of cross-app tracking where offered |

## 6. Decide what the phone is for

This is the part no settings screen covers.

Write a short list: what do you actually want to do on this device? Reading, messaging three specific people, navigation, banking, a camera, music. Then notice how much of your usage falls outside it.

The goal is not less phone. It is a phone that does the things you chose and fewer of the things a growth team chose for you.

<Note title="A realistic experiment">
For one week, charge the phone in a different room overnight. No other changes. That single change — not an app, not a limit — is the one most people report noticing in the morning.
</Note>

## The twice-a-year maintenance pass

Digital hygiene is a small piece of upkeep, like replacing a smoke alarm battery. Put a recurring reminder in the calendar for January and July:

1. Notifications audit — anything new and noisy?
2. App deletion pass — what has not been opened in 30 days?
3. Password manager check for reused or weak entries
4. Permission and privacy review
5. Backups verified, not assumed

Twenty minutes, twice a year. That is the entire system.
